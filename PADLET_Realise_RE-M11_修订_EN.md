**Title:** No Diagram — I Worked the Pins Out in Conversation with an AI

**Body:**

There is no wiring diagram in this project. I want to say that first, because "AI-assisted wiring" sounds like a picture was generated and it was not.

What actually happened is less visual and more ordinary. I had a list of pins on the board and a pile of physical modules on the desk, and I worked out the assignment by talking it through with an AI assistant — which sensor goes on which pin, what voltage each one wants, which ones can share, which pins were already taken. The output was a decision, written into my own notes, not an image.

I am disclosing that the same way AL-P0 discloses how the field interview instrument was built. A tool that shapes the work should be visible in the account of the work, and this one shaped several hours of it.

What it was genuinely good for. It knew that two of my sensors both wanted the same pair of pins and could legitimately share them, because that pair is a shared two-wire connection where each chip answers to a different address — that is the only reason everything fits on a board this small. It knew that the soil moisture module runs on 3.3 volts and not the 5 volts most of the others use, which is the kind of detail that quietly destroys a component. And it was faster at holding the whole pin list in view at once than I was, which is exactly what I wanted from it.

What it got wrong, and what I therefore had to verify myself.

It reasons from the part name I give it. I named the parts from my ideal specification rather than from my desk, so it happily assigned pins for a BME680 and a BH1750. I have a BMP280 and a photoresistor. Everything downstream of those two names had to be redone by hand against the modules I actually own, and RE-I30 is the card where I finally sat down and wrote out the difference between the shopping list and the desk.

It did not know the board was an LGT8F328P clone rather than a genuine Arduino Uno, because I did not know that yet either — see RE-I18. Nothing in the pin assignment was wrong because of it. But every statement it made about that board carried a confidence that turned out to be unearned, and I could not have detected that from the conversation.

And it could not tell me which of the Raspberry Pi's USB-C sockets carries data. That is not a reasoning problem. It is a physical fact about one specific board sitting in front of me, printed in small letters on the board itself, and it cost me an afternoon — RE-I19.

The pattern underneath all three is the same, and it is the reason this card is worth writing. **The AI has never seen my desk.** Everything it says is true of the part as specified, and my build is made of the particular ones I own — a clone chip wearing a famous name, a cheaper sensor standing in for the one in the document, a socket that looks identical to the one next to it. This project keeps running into the same wall from different directions: the specification is not the thing.

So the working rule I ended up with. The tool is fine. The disclosure is mandatory. The verification is mine. Every pin in the final build was confirmed against the physical module in my hand and written into the pin map in RE-S5, and that pin map — not any conversation — is the record.
